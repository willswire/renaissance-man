# Proverbs — The Observed Grain

The regularities Proverbs records, organized for use. These are probabilistic, not mechanical — Proverbs describes how things *tend* to go, and Ecclesiastes (see the companion file) marks where the tendency stops being a promise. This division is by design, not accident: the wisdom corpus deliberately states the general rule in one book and assigns the exceptions to others (Ecclesiastes for wisdom's limits, Job for suffering that maps to nothing). So a proverb quoted alone is a tool removed from its safety guard. Never invert one into a diagnosis: "diligence tends toward sufficiency" does not license "this person's want proves their laziness."

Two framing facts govern everything below. *Chokhmah* is craft-skill — the artisan's word, not the scholar's — so every entry here is a move to practice, not a truth to hold. And the book's stated starting point, the fear of the LORD, is in operational terms humility before a moral order one did not author: the entries below are discovered grain, not house style, which is why they transfer across three thousand years without much translation.

## Speech

The largest single topic in the book, which is itself the datum: wisdom is more threatened by the mouth than by the mind.

- **Volume correlates with error.** In many words, transgression is not lacking; the wise restrain their lips. Practically: the longer the message, the more claims in it, the more that can be wrong, misread, or used against its author. Default shorter.
- **Timing of speech beats content of speech.** A word in season, how good it is — the same sentence lands as help on Tuesday and as attack on Thursday. Before drafting anything hard, the first question is whether now is the season.
- **Softness is a power move, not a concession.** A soft answer turns away wrath; a harsh word stirs anger. The person who lowers the temperature usually sets the direction of the exchange.
- **Answering before hearing is folly and shame.** Restated for modern work: solving the stated problem before confirming it's the real one. The presenting question is often not the question.
- **Gossip and flattery are load-bearing sins.** Both are speech optimized for the speaker's position rather than the truth — one trades on others' failures, one on others' vanity. Both show up constantly in workplace writing (the status update that subtly torpedoes a peer; the review that flatters upward). Refuse both directions.

## Counsel

- **Plans fail for lack of counsel; with many advisers they succeed.** Not a committee mandate — a claim about blind spots. Every plan is drawn from one vantage; counsel is how it acquires depth perception. Operationally: before finalizing any significant recommendation, ask what the person who disagrees would say, and if no real critic is available, simulate the best one.
- **Faithful are the wounds of a friend; profuse are the kisses of an enemy.** The reviewer who marks up the document is doing more for its author than the one who approves it untouched. Comfortable feedback is a warning sign, not a good one.
- **The first to state his case seems right, until the other comes and examines him.** One-sided accounts are structurally persuasive. Never adjudicate — a conflict, an incident, a vendor comparison — on a single party's telling.

## Diligence and the sluggard

- The sluggard passages are comic, and the comedy is diagnostic: the sluggard is not lazy in the sense of doing nothing — he is *always about to start*. He has reasons ("there is a lion outside!"), he desires and gets nothing because desire without initiation is inert, and want arrives "like an armed man" — gradually, then suddenly.
- Modern forms: the refactor perpetually planned, the migration blocked on a risk that is actually an excuse, the "we should really" backlog. The tell is elaborate justification for delay.
- **Small consistent effort compounds.** Go to the ant: no overseer, yet the provision gets made. Steady unsupervised work beats heroic supervised bursts — in savings, fitness, codebases, and relationships.
- The inversion warning applies most strongly here: diligence *tends* toward sufficiency. Reading someone's poverty, failure, or setback as proof of sluggardliness is exactly the inference the wisdom literature as a whole forbids.

## Planning and presumption

- **Planning is a wisdom behavior; its opposite is not spontaneity but haste.** The plans of the diligent tend to abundance; the hasty come to want.
- **The horse is prepared for battle, but the victory is not the horse's.** Preparation is commanded; outcomes are not guaranteed by it. This is the bridge passage to Ecclesiastes. Plain terms: prepare fully, then hold the result loosely — the quality of preparation and the ownership of outcomes are different things.
- **Gathered hastily, it dwindles; little by little, it grows.** Compounding beats windfall — in money, skill, reputation, test coverage, documentation. Be suspicious of any plan whose success depends on a single large discontinuous win.

## Character and reputation

- **A good name over great riches.** Reputation has a longer duration than any position or payout — and unlike most assets, it is lost quickly and rebuilt slowly. In a tradeoff between a short-term win and how the win is achieved, the method is usually the more valuable asset in play.
- **Integrity is operationally simpler.** One story, no version control on the truth. Deception carries a maintenance burden that compounds.
- **The righteous falls seven times and rises.** Character here is not the absence of failure but the response pattern to it. Judge people — including oneself — on the recovery, not the fall.

## Conflict

- **Starting strife is like letting out water** — the beginning is the only cheap moment to stop. The wisdom move is almost always at the first exchange, not the fifth.
- **Keeping aloof from strife is an honor.** Not every fight is worth having, and declining one without needing to win it first is the mark. Most flame-threads, review battles, and framework wars are declinable.
- **Answer / don't answer a fool according to his folly** — the famous adjacent contradiction, and it's deliberate: whether engaging legitimizes nonsense or silence does is a judgment call, pair by pair. The literature refuses to give a rule precisely where a rule would be misused. That refusal is itself instruction: wisdom is the capacity the rules can't replace.
